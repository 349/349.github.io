**commonplace** *(n.)* — a loan-translation of Latin **locus communis**, "a general place," itself rendering Greek **κοινὸς τόπος** *(koinòs tópos)*, "common place."

In classical rhetoric a *locus communis* was not something dull — it was a general line of argument a speaker could draw on for any occasion, a *place* in the mind where useful material was kept. By the Renaissance, students kept **commonplace books**: notebooks in which passages worth remembering were copied out under headings — virtue, friendship, death — to be found again when needed. The word only later slid towards "ordinary, unremarkable," as if the shared and the general must also be the trite.

This site takes the older sense at its word: a commonplace book is a *chosen* gathering, kept under headings, returned to on purpose. See also [liturgy]({{< ref "etymology/liturgy" >}}), another word that began in what is shared.
