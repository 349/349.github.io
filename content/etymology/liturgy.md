**liturgy** *(n.)* — from Greek **λειτουργία** *(leitourgía)*, "public service, a work done for the people," from **λεῖτος** *(leitos)* "of the people" (related to **λαός** *laós*, "people") + **ἔργον** *(érgon)* "work."

In classical Athens a *leitourgia* was a civic obligation: a wealthy citizen would fund a warship, or a chorus for the festival, out of his own pocket — a public work, literally. The religious sense came later, through the Greek of the Septuagint and New Testament, where the word was used for the service of the temple, and then for Christian worship. Latin took it as *liturgia*; it reaches English through French *liturgie* in the sixteenth century.

The bones of the old meaning are still there: worship as *work done in public, for the people* — not a private feeling but a common act. See also [commonplace]({{< ref "etymology/commonplace" >}}) for another word that began as something shared and general before it turned inward.
