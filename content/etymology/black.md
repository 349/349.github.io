**black** *(adj.)* — from Old English **blæc** "black, dark," from Proto-Germanic ***blakaz*** "burned," from the Proto-Indo-European root ***bʰleg-*** "to burn, gleam, flash, shine."

The surprise is what else grows from that root. French **blanc** "white" — borrowed into English as **blank** — comes from Frankish ***blank*** "shining, dazzling, white," from the very same *bʰel-/bʰleg-* family of "shining, burning." So the word for the darkest colour and the word for the brightest are, at the root, one idea: fire. **Black** is what the flame leaves behind — the charred, the burnt-out; **blanc** is the flash of the flame itself, the white of its glare.

English ends up holding both descendants at once: native **black**, the ash, and borrowed **blank**, the shine. The same root flares out into **blaze**, **bleach**, **blond**, and — through Latin and Greek — *flagrāre* "to blaze," *fulgēre* "to shine," and *phlégein* "to burn" (whence *phlegm* and *phlogiston*). A whole vocabulary of light and its residue, from one spark.
